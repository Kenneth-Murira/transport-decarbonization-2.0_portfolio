# EV Adoption and Equity: Evidence from U.S. Household Data

## Context

Electric vehicle (EV) adoption is an important component of transport decarbonization. However, the benefits of policies that accelerate EV adoption may not be distributed equally across households. Understanding which demographic groups are currently overrepresented or underrepresented among EV owners can help policymakers design incentives that expand access to cleaner transportation while addressing potential equity gaps.

This analysis examines the association between household EV/PHEV ownership and three categorical household characteristics: income bracket, geographic region, and household size.

## Key Findings

### Income is the strongest factor associated with EV ownership

Household income had the strongest association with EV ownership among the three factors examined (χ²(4) = 221.0, p < .001, Cramér's V = 0.168).

The standardized residuals show a clear income gradient. Households earning more than $150,000 were substantially overrepresented among EV owners (standardized residual = +13.8), and the same group was substantially underrepresented among non-EV households (−13.8).

In contrast, low-income households (<$25,000), lower-middle-income households ($25,000–$50,000), and middle-income households ($50,000–$100,000) were underrepresented among EV owners relative to what independence would predict, with standardized residuals ranging from −4.7 to −5.5.

The upper-middle-income group ($100,000–$150,000) was comparatively close to the expected distribution.

### Geography also matters, but less than income

Region was significantly associated with EV ownership (χ²(3) = 80.8, p < .001), but the association was weaker than income (Cramér's V = 0.102).

The West was substantially overrepresented among EV owners (standardized residual = +8.8), while the Midwest and South were underrepresented. The Northeast was relatively close to the expected distribution.

### Household size has the weakest association

Household size was statistically associated with EV ownership (χ²(2) = 22.3, p < .001), but the effect was comparatively small (Cramér's V = 0.054).

One-person households were underrepresented among EV owners (standardized residual = −4.0), while households with three or more people were overrepresented (+3.9). Two-person households were close to the expected distribution.

## Policy Implications

The results suggest that existing patterns of EV adoption are disproportionately associated with higher-income households. This raises an equity consideration for EV incentive design: policies that rely primarily on broad consumer subsidies may disproportionately benefit households that are already more likely to adopt EVs.

Policymakers could therefore consider more targeted approaches to reducing the financial barriers faced by lower- and middle-income households. Potential approaches include income-targeted purchase incentives, enhanced rebates for qualifying households, support for used EV purchases, and financing mechanisms that reduce up-front costs.

Geographic targeting may also be appropriate where regional differences reflect differences in charging infrastructure, vehicle availability, electricity markets, or other local conditions. However, the smaller Cramér's V for region indicates that income should receive greater attention than geography when prioritizing equity considerations in this analysis.

## Important Limitation

These results identify statistical associations rather than causal relationships. The analysis does not establish that income, geography, or household size directly causes EV adoption. Other factors, such as vehicle prices, charging availability, access to financing, education, employment, and local policy environments may also influence adoption.

Further analysis using multivariable statistical models would therefore be appropriate before making causal or highly targeted policy recommendations.

## Conclusion

The analysis identifies a clear socioeconomic dimension to early EV adoption. High-income households are substantially overrepresented among EV owners, while lower- and middle-income households are underrepresented. Income has the strongest association among the examined factors (Cramér's V = 0.168), followed by region (0.102) and household size (0.054).

These findings suggest that future EV policies should consider not only increasing the overall rate of adoption, but also improving equitable access to EV technology.
